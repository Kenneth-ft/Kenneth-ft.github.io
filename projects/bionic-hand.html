Electrical Hardware Software / Firmware ★ Featured Project

7DOF Tendon-Driven Bionic Hand

Underactuated, EMG-controlled prosthetic hand for transradial amputees featuring custom power/sensor PCBs and modular C/C++ Hardware Abstraction Layer firmware.

Role Lead Systems Integrator
Organization Engineers Without Borders SFU Bionics
Timeline Jan 2026 – Present
Disciplines PCB Design, Embedded C/C++, HAL Architecture
Tools KiCAD, STM32 / ARM, SolidWorks, SMD Soldering

Project Overview & Systems Architecture

The goal of the EWB SFU Bionics Team is to develop an affordable, lightweight, underactuated 7-degree-of-freedom prosthetic hand for transradial amputees. As Lead Systems Integrator, I drafted the core concept and systems engineering architecture, coordinating efforts across 3 mechanical subteams, 1 electrical team, 1 biomedical team, and 1 software team.

The hand uses tendon-driven actuation driven by high-torque coreless DC servos. Grip patterns are controlled via non-invasive surface electromyography (sEMG) sensors placed on the user's forearm, which capture muscle contraction envelopes.

Electrical Engineering: Custom PCBs

To fit inside the spatial constraints of a human forearm socket, commercial breakout boards were insufficient. I designed, routed, and specified components for custom multi-board PCB assemblies in KiCAD:

  • Power Distribution Board: High-efficiency synchronous buck converters delivering high continuous current (up to 6A peak) to drive multiple micro-servos without browning out the microcontroller.
  • Sensor Multiplexing Board: Routes multi-channel EMG inputs, tendon tension load cells, and magnetic joint encoders back to the main processing unit over a noise-shielded I2C/SPI bus.
  • SMD Assembly & Verification: Hand-soldered 0603 and QFN packages using hot air and microscope inspection, verifying ripple noise on an oscilloscope.

Firmware Architecture: Modular Hardware Abstraction Layer (HAL)

A major architectural bottleneck on past collegiate robotics teams was tight coupling between hardware driver code and high-level logic. If an actuator or sensor model changed, high-level grip algorithms broke.

To eliminate this, I architected a modular Hardware Abstraction Layer (HAL) in C/C++:

  • Driver Layer: Direct register-level access to PWM timers, DMA-driven ADC channels, and hardware I2C/SPI peripherals.
  • HAL Interface: Provides clean standardized interfaces (e.g. Finger_SetTension(id, value), EMG_GetFilteredEnvelope(ch)) completely decoupling hardware from application logic.
  • Control Layer: Finite state machine executing grip transitions (pinch, power grasp, tripod, neutral) based on EMG pattern classification.

Engineering Challenges & Diagnostics

Early breadboard prototypes suffered from severe electrical noise: whenever servo motors actuated, the inductive spikes coupled into the microvolt-level surface EMG analog traces, causing false-trigger grip oscillations.

Using my diagnostic background, I isolated the noise path using an oscilloscope and resolved it through:

  1. Separating noisy motor power planes from sensitive analog ground planes using a single star-ground point.
  2. Implementing LC low-pass pi-filters on the power rails feeding the instrumentation amplifiers.
  3. Adding a 60 Hz digital notch filter and moving-average filter inside the ADC interrupt service routine (ISR).
